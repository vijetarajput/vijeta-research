Feedback on the draft Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management, 2026.

I am writing to offer feedback from ground level experience in exactly the kind of institution this Guidance will apply to. I worked for over five years at a Regional Rural Bank in Gujarat, where I managed 2,500 to 3,000 active loan accounts and supported analytics and reporting across 27 branches. I have since worked in fintech product development, and I now research the oversight of AI systems in lending as an independent researcher.

I welcome the draft Guidance. Extending model risk management to AI and machine learning models and to third party models is timely and necessary. My feedback is on one main theme, with three specific suggestions.

Main theme: the Guidance is strong at the board level, but smaller regulated entities will need operational level guidance to implement it.

The draft rightly places responsibility on the Board and senior management, and asks for a framework, an inventory and validation across the model lifecycle. In my experience, Regional Rural Banks, cooperative banks and smaller NBFCs do not fail at the policy level. They fail at the daily operational level, where policies must become routines. These entities usually have no model validation team and limited technical staff. If implementation expectations are expressed only in board level language, smaller entities are likely to respond with documentation that satisfies the letter of the Guidance without changing daily practice.

My suggestion is that the final Guidance, or an accompanying annex, describe a minimum set of simple operational artefacts that even the smallest regulated entity can maintain. Banking already has good precedents for such artefacts. In my branch work, four ordinary controls kept human lending decisions accountable: a sanctioning authority chain, a fixed pre sanction red flag checklist, early warning monitoring that began at one EMI overdue, and maker checker review of sensitive transactions. Each of these has a direct and inexpensive equivalent for model driven decisions.

Suggestion 1. The model inventory should record a named human owner and the approval of decision cutoffs. I suggest the inventory be required to record, for each model, the named role that owns each decision type the model takes, the cutoffs or thresholds in use, and who approved those cutoffs and when. This restores, in a new form, the old sanctioning question that every banker knows: who sanctioned it. Without a named owner for decisions and cutoffs, accountability diffuses across the entity, the vendor and the data provider, and no single desk answers for outcomes.

Suggestion 2. Ongoing monitoring should include outcome tracking by model version and borrower segment. For lending models, I suggest the Guidance name one concrete minimum monitoring practice: a monthly report of early overdue rates broken down by model version and borrower segment. A segment whose accounts go overdue faster than others is the clearest early warning that a model is scoring that segment wrongly. This practice is inexpensive, because it reuses the NPA and early warning reporting culture that already exists in every bank, including Regional Rural Banks. Any analyst who has prepared NPA reports can prepare this report.

Suggestion 3. High impact model decisions should retain a human review tier, in the spirit of maker checker. Banking has long required that no sensitive transaction is completed by a single actor. I suggest the Guidance apply the same principle to model driven decisions with high customer impact: borderline scores, large ticket sizes and cases carrying fraud or bureau flags should route to a human checker before completion, and the checker's decision should be logged against the model's recommendation. A monthly review of disagreement patterns between checkers and the model is itself a practical validation signal, and it protects the customers least able to contest an automated decision, including low literacy borrowers whom I served directly in rural banking.

These suggestions are deliberately modest. In a system where supervisory capacity and entity level compliance capacity are both stretched, modest and implementable controls will protect customers better than sophisticated requirements that exist only on paper.

The full framework behind these suggestions is set out in my paper, From Prediction to Decision: An Operational Audit Framework for AI Driven Lending, Based on Traditional Banking Controls, available on SSRN, abstract number 7161058. I would be glad to provide any further detail that may be useful.

Thank you for the opportunity to comment.

Vijetasingh Rajput
Independent Researcher, Ahmedabad
